Three microsatellites that launched aboard a SpaceX Falcon 9 from Vandenberg Space Force Base on July 7, 2026 mark the start of "initial operational capability" for FireSat, a wildfire-detection constellation managed by the nonprofit Earth Fire Alliance. After a roughly three-month testing period, the satellites will begin actively feeding data to fire agencies, covering every fire-prone region on Earth at least twice daily.

FireSat bills itself as the first satellite constellation purpose-built to detect wildfires, including the small blazes that other systems tend to miss. The spacecraft were built by California-based Muon Space and have drawn more than $15 million from Google toward initial deployment, plus a $26 million commitment from the Bezos Earth Fund. Each satellite carries multispectral imaging that can see through smoke and clouds and detect fires as small as five by five meters. A FireSat prototype launched in March 2025 collected over a million images and demonstrated it could catch low-intensity fires invisible to existing satellites.

### A build-out toward near-real-time coverage

Early adopters using FireSat data this year include fire agencies in California, Colorado, Australia, and Portugal. As more satellites reach orbit, the program aims to deliver fresh imagery of anywhere on Earth hourly by 2029, and eventually every 20 minutes once the full constellation of more than 50 satellites is deployed in the early 2030s. The Earth Fire Alliance projects that even an hourly revisit rate could save over $1 billion in fire-damage costs, prevent nearly 22 million tons of carbon emissions, and help protect 3,500 homes and 1.3 million acres.

To sharpen detection, Google Research plans to apply its AI models to compare live FireSat data against historical imagery, identifying tiny fires and feeding predictive wildfire modeling. The company framed the launch as "another tangible step forward in putting practical AI to work for climate resilience."

### The uncomfortable trade-off

That optimistic framing sits alongside an awkward reality Ars Technica highlights: the same rush to deploy AI carries steep climate costs. Large AI data centers demand enormous amounts of electricity, increasingly supplied by new natural-gas projects in the U.S. that could collectively emit more than 129 million tons of greenhouse gases annually. Google has acknowledged the difficulty of standing up enough clean energy to offset that demand, noting its company-wide electricity use grew 37 percent in 2025.

### Why it matters

Detection is only one piece of the wildfire problem. Agencies still need the resources to manage ecosystems through prescribed burns and to suppress unwanted fires, work made harder by a warming climate. The launch coincides with severe fires spreading through Canada's boreal forests, forcing thousands in First Nations communities to flee and blanketing more than 100 million people across Canada and the U.S. in hazardous smoke. Faster, more precise detection from orbit could genuinely help, but it underscores a tension at the heart of Big Tech's climate pitch: AI may help fight the fires while the infrastructure behind it adds to the emissions that make them worse.

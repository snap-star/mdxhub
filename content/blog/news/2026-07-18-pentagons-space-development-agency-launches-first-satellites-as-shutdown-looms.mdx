The Pentagon's Space Development Agency is finally launching its first batches of operational satellites, seven years after it was created to move fast and reshape how the military buys space capabilities. The milestone arrives with a heavy dose of irony: it comes just as the Pentagon plans to shut down the semi-autonomous agency and fold its work back into the Space Force's procurement pipeline.

Established in 2019, the SDA was designed to break from the military's traditionally slow acquisition culture. Its central mission has been to build the Proliferated Warfighter Space Architecture (PWSA), a constellation of several hundred missile-warning and data-relay satellites in low-Earth orbit meant to detect, track, and target ballistic and hypersonic missiles. The idea was to replace reliance on a small fleet of legacy missile-warning satellites in much higher geosynchronous orbits, which are expensive and increasingly vulnerable to attack.

### A strategy built on speed

The SDA's approach was to procure and field new "tranches" of satellites every two years, casting a wide net across the US space industry and drawing on hardware and sensors from many different companies. The plan called for a brisk launch cadence of roughly one mission per month. In practice, the agency has struggled to hit that rhythm.

The SDA's history has been marked by schedule delays, production and supply-chain bottlenecks, and technical problems with its first operational data-relay satellites after their launch last year. Those transport satellites are meant to receive tracking data from the missile-warning spacecraft via inter-satellite laser links and relay it to the ground so the military can act on it. Getting that networked architecture working reliably has proven harder than the agency's ambitious timeline assumed.

### Folded into the Space Force

Now the agency itself is being wound down. Lawmakers in both chambers of Congress have backed its closure in drafts of this year's National Defense Authorization Act, so its dissolution is not a surprise. Much of the mission will continue under a different banner within the Space Force, which is being reorganized under several program acquisition executives in an effort to streamline weapons buying.

The missile-warning and data-relay satellites are slated to become part of the Pentagon's planned Golden Dome missile shield, one of the current administration's top priorities. Notably, the capabilities envisioned for the SDA constellation predate the Golden Dome announcement and are far less controversial than the White House's separate push to include space-based weapons in the shield.

### Why it matters

The SDA's arc is a case study in the difficulty of injecting startup-style speed into defense acquisition. The agency delivered real hardware to orbit and helped push a more distributed, resilient satellite architecture, but it never matched the aggressive cadence that justified its independence. As its mission migrates into the Space Force, the open question is whether that consolidation preserves the SDA's faster procurement ethos or reabsorbs it into the very system it was built to disrupt.

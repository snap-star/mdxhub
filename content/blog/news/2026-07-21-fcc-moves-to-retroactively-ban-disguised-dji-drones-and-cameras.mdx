The FCC is preparing to use, for the first time, a power it granted itself last October: the ability to retroactively ban gadgets that have already received its approval to be imported and sold in the United States. The target is a set of "DJI front companies" the agency suspects of sneaking the Chinese company's technology past its foreign drone ban.

### From fines to outright bans

Two Fridays ago, the FCC proposed a $25,000 fine against eight of these alleged front companies, including the ones behind Skyrover drones and Xtra cameras. Now it is going considerably further, proposing to bar those same companies from importing, distributing, marketing, and selling their existing drones and cameras in the US.

The practical effect would be sweeping. The Verge points to Xtra's version of the DJI Osmo Pocket 3 — a product highlighted in a deal just two weeks earlier — which had already cleared FCC approval before the drone ban took effect and ships from Amazon with next-day delivery. If the retroactive ban goes through, products like it would have to disappear from major online retailers and from the companies' own websites, and firms might even have to write off inventory already sitting in US warehouses.

### The broader DJI squeeze

The move is the latest step in a long-running effort to push DJI, the world's largest dronemaker, out of the American market. Regulators have grown increasingly wary of Chinese-made drones and cameras over national-security and data concerns, and the emergence of rebranded or lookalike products has become a focal point of enforcement. By reaching backward to revoke approvals it has already issued, the FCC signals it is willing to disrupt products consumers can buy today, not just future imports.

### Why it matters

For buyers, the proposal raises the prospect that popular, already-approved drones and cameras could vanish from shelves with little warning. For the companies involved, a retroactive ban is far more damaging than a fine, threatening existing revenue and stranded inventory. And for the wider industry, it demonstrates how aggressively US regulators are prepared to act against hardware they believe is Chinese technology in disguise — a precedent that could reshape how foreign electronics reach the American market.

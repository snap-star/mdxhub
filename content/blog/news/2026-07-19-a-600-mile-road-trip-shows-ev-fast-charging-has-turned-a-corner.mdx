Charging anxiety still looms large for prospective EV buyers, with just over half of AAA survey respondents last year citing public charging infrastructure as a key concern. Those worries have been well earned: fast charging has historically been unreliable enough that TechCrunch's Tim De Chant once drafted an EV fast-charging "bill of rights" after a disastrous 2023 road trip. So he was genuinely surprised when a recent 600-mile summer trip to Montreal went, with one minor exception, flawlessly.

### The trip that just worked

The plan had been to take a Kia EV9 rated for nearly 300 miles per charge, but with the Kia sidelined by a broken air conditioner, the family instead drove an Audi e-tron good for only about 220 miles. Despite the shorter range, the smaller-battery car handled the journey without drama, a reminder that maximizing range isn't a prerequisite for a smooth trip.

To plan stops, De Chant used A Better Route Planner (ABRP), an app that optimizes charging by factoring in wind, temperature, vehicle specs, and battery degradation. It routed him first to a Rivian charging site near Lebanon, New Hampshire, where he found no lines, six working 300-kilowatt stalls, and plenty of food options nearby. The charger accepted a credit card directly and delivered around 140 kilowatts, roughly the e-tron's ceiling, and the return trip through the same site went just as smoothly.

### One small hitch

The only stumble came at a Circuit Électrique station outside Montreal, where the card reader failed and he had to download the operator's app and preload 20 Canadian dollars before charging proceeded normally. Even that stop, he admits, wasn't strictly necessary given light local driving and a working hotel charger. Each session lasted roughly 20 minutes and was paired with a meal or rest stop, so the family never actually waited on the car. All told, the three charging sessions combined took about as long as the wait at the U.S. border on the way home.

### Why it matters

The account is a single anecdote, but it lines up with a broader shift in the charging landscape that would have been hard to imagine three years ago. Reliable hardware, working credit-card readers, smarter route planning, and better station siting collectively address many of the pain points that soured early adopters. Public charging remains uneven, and the app-and-preload friction at one stop shows the experience isn't fully seamless yet. Still, for buyers weighing whether an EV can handle a long trip, the takeaway is encouraging: the infrastructure has quietly improved to the point where a multi-hundred-mile drive can feel routine rather than an ordeal.

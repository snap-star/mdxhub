When Sam Altman invited author Dave Eggers to address roughly 200 OpenAI employees last year, the company might have expected an inspiring talk on creativity and productivity from one of the most prolific figures in modern letters. Eggers has written novels, screenplays, and journalism, founded McSweeney's, and started schools and nonprofits supporting writers. Instead, according to the Financial Times as reported by The Verge, he used the platform to criticize the very product the room was building.

### A pointed message

Eggers told the assembled staff that ChatGPT's effect on educators has been "catastrophic," saying the tool had made every teacher's job dramatically harder than it was two years earlier. His sharpest concern centered on students who lean on the chatbot to write for them. If they use it to compose, he argued, they will never learn to write, and their own voice will effectively be taken from them. Losing the ability to "say their truth and tell their own story," he said, amounts to "silencing an entire generation or two."

### Altman likely knew what was coming

As The Verge notes, none of this should have blindsided Altman. Eggers is a longtime skeptic of Silicon Valley's ambitions; his best-selling novel *The Circle* is a scathing send-up of a data-hungry tech giant, and he has dismissed AI-generated writing as "pastiche nonsense." Inviting him was, in a sense, inviting the critique.

### Why it matters

The episode captures a growing unease about generative AI's role in classrooms that even the industry's own gatherings can't fully sidestep. Educators have spent the past couple of years wrestling with how to detect AI-assisted work, whether to ban or embrace it, and how to keep students actually learning core skills when a tool can produce a passable essay in seconds. Eggers frames the stakes not as academic cheating but as something more fundamental: the development of a personal voice, which he sees as inseparable from the hard practice of writing.

What makes the moment notable is the venue. A prominent cultural figure stood inside OpenAI and told its staff, to their faces, that their flagship product may be harming a generation's ability to express itself. Whether or not one accepts his conclusions, the confrontation highlights a question the company cannot easily answer with better models or new features: what happens to human skill and creativity when the machine becomes the default first draft. For a company racing to embed its tools ever deeper into schools and workplaces, that is a critique that lands close to home.

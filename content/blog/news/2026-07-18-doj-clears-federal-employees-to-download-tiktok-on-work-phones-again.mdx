Federal employees can once again install TikTok on their government-issued devices, according to the Department of Justice. The reversal, reported by Reuters, unwinds a restriction that had kept the short-form video app off official phones for years and reflects the app's dramatically changed ownership situation in the United States.

A 2022 law barred federal workers from using TikTok on government devices, part of a broader wave of concern about the app's ties to its Chinese parent company. The DOJ now reportedly takes the position that the law no longer applies, pointing to a deal that transferred TikTok's US operations to a new joint venture. That venture is backed by Oracle, Silver Lake, and MGX, with Oracle serving as the security partner. Previous owner ByteDance retains a 19.9 percent stake in the arrangement.

### What the memo says

According to the report, a DOJ memo states that President Donald Trump has cleared "employees of Executive Branch agencies" to "download TikTok onto their official devices, subject to the agency's discretion and consistent with all applicable workplace policies." In other words, the blanket prohibition is being lifted, but individual agencies still have latitude to set their own rules. Employees will not automatically be free to install the app everywhere; each agency can decide whether and how it fits within existing security and workplace guidelines.

### The bigger picture

The policy shift is the latest twist in a long and unusually winding saga over TikTok's future in the US. After the original ban aimed at government devices, the app faced a broader nationwide restriction. But as that law took effect early last year, TikTok went dark only briefly before the administration repeatedly delayed enforcement and pressed service providers to restore access. The eventual ownership restructuring, moving US operations under a domestic, Oracle-anchored consortium, is what the DOJ now cites as the basis for treating the earlier prohibition as moot.

### Why it matters

The change signals how much the security calculus around TikTok has shifted once its US operations were placed under domestic control with a dedicated security partner. For the government, the restructured ownership appears to have addressed enough of the original data and national-security concerns to justify lifting the device ban. For TikTok, having federal employees back on the platform is another marker of normalization after years of existential uncertainty.

Practically speaking, the impact will vary agency by agency, since each retains discretion over its own policies. Some may embrace the change quickly, while others could keep tighter controls in place. But symbolically, the reversal underscores that the fierce debate over TikTok in the US has moved from outright bans toward managed access under new ownership, a resolution that would have seemed unlikely at the height of the controversy.

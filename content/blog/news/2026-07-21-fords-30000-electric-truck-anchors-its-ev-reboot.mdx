The end of the F-150 Lightning also marked the start of a new chapter for Ford. After failing to win the market with an electric version of its best-selling F-Series truck, the automaker is starting over with its Universal Electric Vehicle (UEV) Platform — and its first offering will be a $30,000 electric truck engineered above all for efficiency and profitability.

### A deliberately small truck

The truck is set to be revealed in 2027 and built at Ford's Kentucky assembly plant, which will eventually produce a whole family of low-cost EVs on the platform. Heavily camouflaged prototypes have already been spotted in the wild. The Autopian caught one in Long Beach and estimated it at roughly 64 inches tall and 195 inches long, making it slightly smaller than a Ford Maverick — unusually compact for a modern pickup. New prototype photos from The Drive reveal real door handles and a large interior touchscreen, along with playful camouflage featuring soccer balls, sailboats, and hearts.

### Recovering from a costly retreat

Ford has struggled to make money on EVs, taking a staggering $19.5 billion writedown on its electric investments in 2025. That reckoning led the company to discontinue several electric models — including the well-regarded F-150 Lightning — and scrap plans for a next-generation electric truck codenamed T3 and an electric commercial van. The broader climate hasn't helped, with loosening emissions rules, tariffs, and the elimination of the federal EV tax credit all weighing on demand. In the near term, Ford is leaning on hybrids, extended-range EVs, and its profitable gas-powered lineup.

### A leadership shake-up

The reset comes with changes at the top. Doug Field, who left Apple five years ago to lead Ford's multibillion-dollar EV and software push, is stepping down. Alan Clarke, a former Tesla engineer who runs Ford's California skunkworks lab, is being promoted to vice president of advanced development projects and will continue steering the UEV platform.

### Why it matters

Ford still views EVs as the future — just not the oversize, unprofitable kind. By anchoring its comeback on a small, affordable truck built for margins rather than headlines, the 122-year-old company is testing whether cheaper, right-sized electric vehicles can finally make the math work. If the $30,000 target holds, it could reshape how legacy automakers approach the mass-market EV.

Anthropic can finally begin paying out the group of authors and book publishers who sued it for copyright infringement. On Monday, a federal judge granted final approval to the AI lab's $1.5 billion class-action settlement, according to a Reuters report. Judge William Alsup of the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California had issued preliminary approval last year after finding that Anthropic illegally downloaded and stored millions of copyrighted books. Alsup has since retired, and Judge Araceli Martinez-Olguin signed the final order.

### How the money breaks down

The agreement pays roughly $3,000 per work across an estimated 500,000 titles, split among the authors and publishers who hold the rights. It is believed to be the largest settlement in the history of U.S. copyright law. Even so, many creators do not see it as a clear victory — largely because of how the underlying legal questions were resolved.

### A split ruling that still stands

Alsup sided with Anthropic on the central issue, ruling that training an AI model on copyrighted text qualifies as fair use, a decision widely viewed as a turning point for the industry. What the ruling did not excuse was how Anthropic acquired the books. The company built its training library from two sources: books it bought and scanned, which the court treated as acceptable, and books pulled from pirate repositories such as Library Genesis and Pirate Library Mirror. Alsup found that second method unlawful and said the piracy question could proceed to trial. Anthropic settled shortly afterward, avoiding both a jury verdict and any damages that might have come with it.

### Why it matters

Because Alsup's decision was a single district-court ruling, and because Anthropic chose to settle rather than appeal, the case will never reach an appellate court to become binding precedent. That leaves the broader legal question unsettled: other judges remain free to weigh the same issues differently based on the facts before them.

That is already playing out. A string of copyright suits continues against companies including Google, Meta, Midjourney, and OpenAI over whether training on copyrighted material is lawful. Just last week, a group of publishers and authors — among them Hachette, Cengage, Elsevier, and author Scott Turow — filed a class action accusing Google of using their copyrighted works to train its Gemini platform. Anthropic's payout closes one high-profile chapter, but the fight over how AI models are trained is far from over.

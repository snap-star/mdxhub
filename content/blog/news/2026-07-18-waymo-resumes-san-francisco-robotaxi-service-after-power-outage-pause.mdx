Waymo temporarily halted its driverless ride-hailing service across San Francisco during a power outage that reportedly affected roughly 7,000 PG&E customers, then restored normal operations after about an hour. The Alphabet-owned company framed the disruption as a precautionary step rather than a technical failure, saying it made "temporary adjustments" while it assessed conditions on the ground.

According to a screenshot shared on social media, riders were told that service had been "temporarily paused" and that freeway routes were unavailable during the outage. A Waymo spokesperson later confirmed the company chose to suspend rides for approximately one hour so it could gauge the scale of the blackout and coordinate with local officials before bringing cars back online. "We know riders depend on us, and we will return to normal operations as soon as possible," the spokesperson said, later confirming that service had indeed resumed.

### Why it matters

The episode highlights a recurring tension for autonomous vehicles operating at city scale: how they behave when the surrounding infrastructure fails. Robotaxis rely on a web of signals, connectivity, and traffic systems, all of which can wobble when the power grid goes down. Pausing service may be the safest response, but it also underscores how sensitive large fleets are to conditions well outside their own hardware and software.

This is not the first time an outage has tripped up Waymo in San Francisco. The company previously saw vehicles stall on city streets during a December blackout, and a separate incident reportedly contributed to traffic snarls during a Fourth of July fireworks event near the Golden Gate Bridge. Each disruption adds to a growing record that regulators and city leaders are watching closely.

### The regulatory backdrop

San Francisco Mayor Daniel Lurie has called for stronger state rules that specifically address how autonomous vehicles should operate during major incidents, whether planned or unexpected. As robotaxis become a more visible part of the city's transportation mix, officials want clearer expectations for what companies do when the grid, the weather, or a large public event throws normal traffic patterns into chaos.

For Waymo, the quick recovery is a talking point in its favor: rather than leaving cars stranded, it proactively pulled them back and then resumed once it understood the situation. But the incident also feeds the broader debate about accountability and coordination between private AV operators and the cities they serve. As more of these vehicles hit the road, the friction points are less about whether the technology can drive and more about how it fits into the messy, unpredictable reality of urban infrastructure.

The takeaway is that resilience, not just capability, will increasingly define public trust in driverless services. Handling a routine day of clear roads is one thing; gracefully managing a citywide outage is another, and it is exactly those edge cases that shape regulation and rider confidence going forward.

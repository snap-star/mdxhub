The US Space Force announced on Friday that it is tripling the maximum value of one of its National Security Space Launch (NSSL) contracts, from $5.6 billion to $17 billion. The expansion comes as the Pentagon signals rising demand for military satellite launches. The NSSL program lets Space Systems Command, which oversees the Space Force's launch efforts, pick from a pool of providers for individual missions delivering military satellites to orbit.

### Two lanes, two risk profiles

The program is split into two parts. Lane 1 handles more risk-tolerant missions — medium-lift launches with experimental payloads or rideshare flights carrying surveillance and data-relay satellites — and is open to commercial providers without extensive certification. This is the lane the Pentagon just authorized for $17 billion. Lane 2 covers higher-priority strategic missions, such as the government's largest spy satellites and radiation-hardened communications satellites designed to survive a nuclear war. Only SpaceX's Falcon 9 and Falcon Heavy and United Launch Alliance's Vulcan are currently certified for Lane 2.

More companies qualify for Lane 1. Space Systems Command initially chose SpaceX, ULA, and Blue Origin in the 2024 Phase 3 round, later adding Rocket Lab, Stoke Space, and most recently Relativity Space and Impulse Space. The Space Force issues bids for a set of Lane 1 missions each year and awards fixed-price task orders to winners. SpaceX has taken the lion's share so far, while Blue Origin won its first earlier this year.

### Demand climbing fast

The original plan pegged Lane 1 at roughly 30 task orders over five years, with a $5.6 billion ceiling; Lane 2 was capped at $13.7 billion last year to cover an estimated 54 launches through 2029. But projections have shifted quickly. In April, Space Systems Command said it had identified 25 additional Lane 2 missions on top of the original 54. With Friday's Lane 1 increase, the combined ceiling for both lanes now tops $30 billion — and officials suggest it could rise again.

### Why it matters

Behind the numbers are ambitious Pentagon programs driving the surge, including multibillion-dollar SpaceX awards for the Space Data Network and Airborne Moving Target Indicator constellations, and Golden Dome, the administration's proposed missile-defense shield expected to rely on space-based sensors and interceptors. The contract expansion underscores how central commercial launch providers have become to US national security — and how much money is flowing toward getting military hardware into orbit.

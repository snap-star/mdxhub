To mark World Emoji Day, Google is making its Noto 3D emoji set available to everyone and open-sourcing the collection so designers and developers can use the little pictograms in their own projects. Alongside the release, the company shared a look at how it approached the design process, revealing that translating familiar 2D emoji into three dimensions raises questions most people never think about.

Turning a flat illustration into a 3D model forces decisions that simply do not come up in two dimensions. Google pointed to the humble smiley face as an example: should it be rendered as a sphere, a mask, or a flat disc? Details that are trivial to ignore in a 2D drawing suddenly become structural choices when there is depth, lighting, and geometry to account for. Those seemingly small calls ripple across an entire set that needs to feel consistent and recognizable.

### Why open-sourcing matters

The bigger news for the design and developer community is that Google is fully open-sourcing the emoji set rather than keeping it locked to its own products. That means anyone can incorporate the 3D emoji into their own creations, from apps and websites to presentations and illustrations. Open-sourcing a polished, professionally designed asset library lowers the barrier for smaller teams and independent creators who might not have the resources to build their own 3D iconography from scratch.

It also fits a broader pattern of companies releasing design assets and tooling to the wider ecosystem. When a well-known set of emoji becomes freely available and modifiable, it can seed consistency across countless third-party projects while giving creators a trustworthy starting point. The move effectively turns Google's internal design work into a shared resource.

### Design as storytelling

Beyond the practical release, Google used the occasion to offer a behind-the-scenes narrative about the craft involved. Emoji have become a near-universal visual language, and the effort to render them in 3D is a reminder that even the smallest interface elements carry real design intent. The company framed the project as both a technical exercise and a celebration of how much thought goes into symbols people use every day without a second glance.

### Why it matters

For everyday users, the immediate effect is more expressive, higher-fidelity emoji that could show up in more places over time. For designers and developers, the open-source release is the headline: a ready-made, cohesive 3D emoji set they can freely adopt and adapt. Timed to World Emoji Day, the announcement blends a bit of celebration with a genuinely useful gift to the creative community, and it underscores how open-sourcing quality assets can extend a company's design influence far beyond its own platforms.
